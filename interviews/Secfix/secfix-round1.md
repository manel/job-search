# 4. Main case study: Dataset Manager

## Use this first if they ask about a project you owned.

### 30-second setup

> At Abacum, I joined the integrations and datasets squad as the first dedicated designer. The team had a clear business problem: finance teams were dependent on implementation whenever they needed to configure or change their data.
>
> The existing experience was essentially a JSON editor, so the obvious solution seemed to be building a UI around it. But customer research showed that the deeper problem wasn't just the interface — the product was exposing the technical model in a language that didn't match how finance teams thought about their data.

### The key decision

> The key realization was that we weren't really solving a UI problem. We were solving an abstraction problem.
>
> We needed to create a layer between the underlying technical system and the user's mental model, while still respecting the constraints of the existing product.

### Tradeoff

> We deliberately limited some of the flexibility of the underlying system because datasets fed other parts of the product. Giving users unrestricted control could create invalid configurations and downstream problems.
>
> So we chose guardrails over maximum flexibility.

### Engineering collaboration

> Engineering was involved throughout. We had to understand what could safely change, what needed to remain compatible, and where we could introduce constraints.
>
> I wasn't designing in isolation and then handing everything over.

### Outcome

> Customers became much more self-sufficient. The implementation team estimated roughly half the time for standard dataset configuration, and configuration-related support requests also decreased.
>
> Customers could reach a working configuration in their first session rather than needing follow-up implementation work.

### Important honesty

The full formula-based solution wasn't completed while you owned the squad.

If asked:

> I eventually moved to the engine squad, so the longer-term transformation layer wasn't completely finished under my ownership. But we had established the foundation and the direction for the next stage.

**Don't hide this.** The fact that you can explain what was and wasn't completed makes the story more credible.

# 6. Likely questions + your angle

### "How do you start on a new problem?"

> I first try to understand the business context, the user problem and the constraints. I identify what we know, what we don't know, and which assumptions are risky. Then I work with the team to agree on the actual problem before exploring solutions.

---

### "How do you work with engineers?"

> I prefer bringing engineers into the conversation early, especially when there are technical dependencies. I don't see engineering as the final step after design. It's usually a shared problem-solving process.

---

### "How do you deal with ambiguity?"

> I try to turn ambiguity into explicit questions: what do we know, what don't we know, which assumptions are risky, and what decision do we actually need to make? That usually makes the problem much more manageable.

---

### "How do you prioritize?"

Think:

**User value + business value + risk + effort + dependencies**

Don't make it sound like a purely numerical framework.

---

### "What would you do differently on Dataset Manager?"

> I would have pushed earlier for a clearer shared language across the datasets and modeling layers. We identified that problem during the work, but it became even clearer when I later moved to the engine squad and worked on the modeling syntax.

---

### "You don't have security/compliance experience. Is that a concern?"

> I haven't worked specifically in security compliance, but I'm comfortable entering complex domains and learning the underlying model. A lot of my experience has involved products where the domain logic is complex, and that's actually one of the things I enjoy.

---

### "Where do you want to go in your career?"

> I'm primarily interested in growing as a senior individual contributor. I enjoy having a lot of ownership over complex product problems and influencing product direction, without necessarily moving into people management.
